1The short answer

An insurance broker is a professional who helps people and businesses buy the right insurance. The broker works for the customer, not for the insurance company. They find out what could go wrong, shop around several insurers, recommend the best cover, and help when it is time to make a claim.

Insurance brokerage (or "broking", the usual Australian word) is simply the business of doing this.

Think of it like

A mortgage broker. You do not go to every bank yourself. The mortgage broker compares many banks' home loans and helps you pick one. An insurance broker does the same thing, but for insurance.

2First, what insurance is

Insurance is a deal: you pay a company a small, certain amount of money now (the premium), and in return it promises to pay for a large, uncertain loss if it happens (a claim).

It works because the insurer collects premiums from many customers, and only a few of them have a loss in any year. The many pay for the few.

Example 1: car insurance

You pay $1,200 a year. Someone crashes into your car and the repair costs $9,000. The insurer pays the repairer. You pay only the agreed first part of the claim (the excess), say $800.

Example 2: a café

A café pays $4,000 a year for business insurance. A kitchen fire forces it to close for six weeks. The insurer pays to repair the kitchen and also covers the income it lost while closed.

3What a broker is

A broker is a middle person between the customer and the insurance companies. The key point is whose side they are on: a broker acts for the customer.

A broker does not pay claims out of their own pocket. The insurance company carries the risk and pays. The broker gives advice, arranges the policy and fights for the customer.

Example 1: a buyer's agent

When buying a house, a buyer's agent works for the buyer, not the seller. They search, compare and negotiate on your behalf. A broker plays that role in insurance.

Example 2: a plumbing business

Sam runs a plumbing business with five vans. Sam calls a broker once. The broker sorts out insurance for the vans, the tools, the workers and the risk of damaging a client's home. Sam deals with one person instead of ten insurers.

4The three main players

Most insurance sales involve three parties. This picture shows how they connect, and which way the money flows.

Broker vs agent. An agent usually sells for one insurer, like a salesperson for that brand. A broker can shop across many insurers for the customer. Example: a car dealer offering "our partner's" insurance is acting like an agent; a broker would compare five insurers' quotes for you.

5What a broker actually does

A broker's work repeats every year. These are the main steps.

  1. Understand the customer's risks Ask questions to find what could go wrong and how much it would cost.
    Example

    For a café: fire, theft, a customer slipping on a wet floor, food poisoning, the fridge breaking and spoiling stock.

  2. Shop the market Send the details (a "submission") to several insurers and collect quotes.
    Example

    A builder's broker gets quotes from three insurers: $18,000, $21,500 and $16,900. The cheapest one leaves out flood damage, so it is not automatically the best.

  3. Recommend and explain Compare price and what is covered, then explain the trade-offs in plain words.
    Example

    "Option B costs $1,500 more, but it covers you if a flood shuts the site. Given you're near the river, I'd take it."

  4. Place the cover Once the customer chooses, the broker arranges the policy, collects the premium and sends it on to the insurer. This is called "placing" the risk.
  5. Help with claims When something goes wrong, the broker helps fill in the claim and pushes the insurer to pay fairly and quickly.
    Example

    A storm floods a shop. The broker sends photos and invoices to the insurer, chases progress, and challenges a low offer.

  6. Renew every year Most policies last 12 months. Before renewal, the broker checks what has changed and shops the market again.
    Example

    The plumbing business bought two more vans and hired three staff, so its cover needs to grow.

6Who uses a broker

Most people buy simple insurance (car, home contents) online themselves. Brokers matter most when the risk is bigger, more complex or unusual.

Small business

Most common

Cafés, tradies, shops, small offices. They need several policies at once and have no time to compare.

Example: a hair salon needing building, contents, liability and staff cover.

Large company

Complex needs

Big risks that one insurer may not take alone, often spread over many insurers.

Example: a mining company insuring a $500m processing plant.

Individuals

Less common

People with higher-value or unusual items.

Example: a family with a prestige home, a boat and an art collection.

7How brokers make money

Brokers mainly earn in two ways:

  • Commission: the insurer pays the broker a share of the premium, often somewhere around 10% to 20% for business insurance (it varies by product and insurer).
  • Fees: the broker charges the customer directly for their advice and work, either on top of or instead of commission.
Example: one café policy
$10,000premium the café pays
$1,50015% commission the insurer pays the broker
+ $300broker fee charged to the café

The broker earns $1,800 for that client this year. Multiply across thousands of clients, and repeat every renewal.

Some brokers also earn interest by holding premiums briefly before passing them to insurers, or by lending customers the money to pay their premium in instalments ("premium funding").

Why this matters: brokers win when premiums rise (bigger commission) and when clients stay year after year. Because commission comes from the insurer, brokers in Australia must tell customers how they are paid, so the customer can judge any conflict of interest.

8Why not just buy direct?

You can, and for simple things many people do. A broker earns their keep when:

  • The fine print matters. Example: two policies look the same, but one excludes damage from "gradual leaks". A broker spots that before you need it.
  • A claim is disputed. Example: after a burglary, the insurer says the alarm was not set. The broker argues the case with evidence.
  • The risk is too big or odd for one insurer. Example: insuring a concert tour or a cargo ship. The broker splits the risk across several insurers.
  • Time. Example: a business owner would rather run the business than spend days comparing 15 policy wordings.
